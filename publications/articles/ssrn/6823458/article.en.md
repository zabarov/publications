Abstract
This paper introduces the State Tone Index (STI), a conceptual-methodological framework for
organizing indicators of macro-social affective climate and institutional-emotional conditions at the
country level. The paper responds to a measurement gap: existing comparative indicators separately
measure wellbeing, governance, rights, trust, social cohesion, press freedom and fragility, but rarely
provide a transparent construct map linking these domains.

The proposed framework draws on literature on emotional climate, collective emotions, social cohesion,
social trust, institutional trust, legitimacy and composite indicators. STI is treated as a
multidimensional framework rather than a direct measure of a single national emotion. The current v0
pilot uses accessible public proxy indicators from the World Happiness Report, Worldwide Governance
Indicators, Freedom House and Reporters Without Borders, with Transparency International CPI
retained as a contextual source outside the baseline score.

The paper contributes a construct map, indicator-role logic and reproducible pilot workflow. The
exploratory v0 demonstration separates wellbeing/agency, governance/openness and
rights/information dimensions, then tests whether baseline rankings are sensitive to alternative
weighting and exclusion scenarios. The demonstration shows why decomposition and rank-sensitivity
review are more defensible than a single headline country ranking.

The current STI v0 is not validated and does not directly measure emotional climate. It excludes direct
social-trust survey variables, emotional-climate scale items, media-sentiment measures, conflict-event
data and direct polarization indicators. The pilot is therefore best interpreted as a proxy-condition and

1. Introduction
International comparisons of national condition usually separate economic performance, subjective
wellbeing, governance quality, civil liberties, press freedom, social trust and social conflict into different
measurement systems. This separation is analytically useful, but it makes it difficult to describe how
institutional conditions, social cohesion and collective affect interact as a broader state-level climate
(OECD & Joint Research Centre, 2008; Schiefer & van der Noll, 2017).

Research on emotional climate and collective emotions shows that societies can be studied through
shared affective orientations and group-level emotional processes (Bar-Tal et al., 2007; De Rivera &
Paez, 2007; Goldenberg et al., 2020; von Scheve & Ismer, 2013). Related literatures on social cohesion,
social trust, institutional trust and legitimacy show that the emotional and relational condition of a
political community is not reducible to income, regime type or one institutional score (Chan et al.,
2006; Levi & Stoker, 2000; Schiefer & van der Noll, 2017). At the same time, composite-indicator
methodology warns that aggregation without explicit construct boundaries, normalization, weighting
and sensitivity analysis can create misleading rankings (OECD & Joint Research Centre, 2008; Saisana
et al., 2005).

Existing indicators capture important fragments of national condition, but they rarely provide a
transparent framework for connecting macro-social affective climate with cohesion, trust, legitimacy,
information openness and governance conditions. The gap addressed here is therefore not the absence
of data, but the absence of a reproducible construct map and decomposition workflow that can organize
these fragments without presenting them as a single validated diagnosis.

This paper introduces the State Tone Index (STI) as a conceptual-methodological framework for
organizing measurable indicators of macro-social affective climate and institutional-emotional
conditions. The current v0 pilot measures proxy conditions around state tone rather than emotional
climate directly. The contribution is fourfold:

    define state tone as a multidimensional construct rather than a literal national emotion;
    map emotional climate, collective emotions, social cohesion, trust, legitimacy, rights, information
    openness and governance into distinct construct layers;
    propose a transparent composite-index workflow with explicit normalization, weighting and
    sensitivity requirements;
    demonstrate an exploratory v0 pilot as a decomposition exercise, not as a validated ranking.

The research question is: Can macro-social affective climate be operationalized as a transparent
multidimensional framework that integrates emotional climate, collective emotions, social cohesion,

2. Literature Review
The first foundation for STI is the literature on emotional climate and collective emotions. Paez et
al. (1997) and De Rivera and Paez (2007) support the idea that emotional climate can be treated as a
social and cross-cultural object of study rather than only as an individual psychological state. Bar-Tal et
al. (2007) connect collective emotions to societal processes in conflict situations, while von Scheve and
Ismer (2013) and Goldenberg et al. (2020) provide broader theoretical and review-level support for
collective emotions as group-level phenomena.

For STI, this literature justifies the conceptual layer of macro-social affective climate. It does not, by
itself, validate a country-level composite score. This paper therefore uses the literature to justify the
construct and separately treats measurement as an explicit methodological problem.

The second foundation is social cohesion. Chan et al. (2006), Schiefer and van der Noll (2017), and
Fonseca et al. (2019) show that social cohesion has been defined and operationalized in multiple ways,
usually involving connectedness, belonging, shared orientation, cooperation and social relations. STI
treats social cohesion as a distinct construct layer. Schiefer and van der Noll’s (2017) three-part
organization around social relations, identification with a geographical unit and orientation toward the
common good is especially useful for this framework. Fonseca et al. (2019) add a multi-level view that
connects individual belonging, community relationships and institutional inclusion. STI does not
equate social cohesion with political conformity, absence of dissent, equality, quality of life or simple
regime stability.

The third foundation is trust. Putnam (1995) frames social capital and civic connectedness as important
properties of societal functioning. Hu (2017) and Justwan et al. (2018) add measurement caution by
showing that trust is not a single simple quantity: generalized trust, radius of trust and aggregate trust
measures can differ conceptually and empirically. For STI, future WVS or equivalent survey variables
need careful construct mapping. Generalized trust, radius of trust, confidence in institutions, social
support and interpersonal trust should not be merged without construct justification.

The fourth foundation is institutional trust and legitimacy. Levi and Stoker (2000) review political trust
and trustworthiness. De Cremer and Tyler (2007) connect trust in authority and procedural fairness to
cooperation, and Hamm et al. (2017) develop an integrated framework linking fair process, trust and
legitimacy. Valgardsson et al. (2025) adds a current cross-national frame for institutional-trust trends.
STI separates institutional trust and legitimacy from social trust and from objective governance
capacity.

The methodological foundation is OECD and Joint Research Centre (2008) and Saisana et al. (2005).
Composite-indicator construction requires transparent construct definition, indicator selection,
normalization, weighting, imputation, robustness and sensitivity analysis. STI follows this logic by
emphasizing construct boundaries, dimension scores, source limitations and rank sensitivity rather
than a single headline score.

3. Methodology
This study is a conceptual-methodological working paper with an exploratory data demonstration. It
proposes a State Tone Index framework and demonstrates a reproducible pilot workflow, but it does not
claim validation, prediction or policy diagnosis.

STI is defined as a multidimensional framework for macro-social affective climate and institutional-
emotional conditions. The construct map separates emotional climate, collective emotions, social
cohesion, social trust, institutional trust, legitimacy, information openness, rights conditions,
governance capacity and wellbeing/agency. In this version, social cohesion is treated as a relational
construct involving social relations, belonging/identification and orientation toward the common good;
social trust is separated from institutional confidence; and legitimacy is treated as connected to
institutional trust and procedural fairness.

The current v0 pilot uses only accessible public proxy variables. It contains three practical dimensions:

  1. Wellbeing/agency: life evaluation, social support and freedom to make life choices from the World
     Happiness Report (World Happiness Report, 2025).

The workflow defines construct layers, maps candidate indicators, excludes circular or unavailable
indicators, normalizes variables so that higher values indicate more favorable proxy conditions,
computes dimension scores before aggregate scores, compares alternative weighting scenarios and flags
rank instability. Exploratory country outputs are interpreted only as method/decomposition examples.


4. Framework Demonstration
The main output is a framework demonstration rather than a validated empirical result. STI v0
organizes country-level proxy indicators into three exploratory dimensions: wellbeing/agency,
governance/openness and rights/information. The baseline score averages the three dimension scores.
Alternative scores test whether results change when variables are weighted equally, when rights
indicators are reduced, or when wellbeing variables are reduced.

The v0 output identifies a high-scoring group in the baseline specification, including Finland, Denmark,
Norway, Sweden and New Zealand, and a low-scoring group including Kenya, China, Nigeria, the
Russian Federation and Turkiye. These positions are not country diagnoses. They reflect how the
selected proxy variables behave under the baseline construction.

Two dimension-divergent cases, Mexico and the United Arab Emirates, were flagged by rank-sensitivity
checks. Mexico appears more favorable on wellbeing/agency than on governance and
rights/information proxies. The United Arab Emirates appears stronger on governance/wellbeing
proxies than on rights/information proxies. These cases support the argument that decomposition is
necessary: a single score can obscure dimension-specific patterns.

5. Discussion
The STI v0 demonstration supports a narrow methodological interpretation: country-level proxy
indicators can be organized into a multidimensional framework that separates wellbeing/agency,
governance/openness and rights/information conditions. The most important finding is not the rank
order itself, but the behavior of the framework under decomposition and alternative weighting choices.

The divergent cases identified in the v0 sensitivity review illustrate why a single composite score is not
enough. Countries may appear relatively stronger on one dimension and weaker on another. This is
central to the STI argument: state tone should be interpreted through a profile of conditions, not
through a headline score.

The framework is consistent with literature that treats emotional climate and collective emotions as
social phenomena, but the current pilot does not directly measure them. It also draws from social-
cohesion and trust literatures, but current v0 data do not yet include direct WVS or equivalent trust
variables. Methodologically, the approach follows composite-indicator guidance by making indicator
selection, weighting and sensitivity review explicit.

The main limitations are that STI v0 measures proxy conditions, not emotional climate directly; direct
trust, cohesion, legitimacy, polarization, media-sentiment and conflict-event variables are excluded;
several input sources are themselves composite or perception-based indices; WGI, Freedom House,
RSF and CPI may overlap conceptually; weighting and normalization choices remain subjective; no
external validation has been conducted; and cross-cultural and language biases remain unresolved.


6. Conclusion
This paper proposes the State Tone Index as a multidimensional framework for organizing measurable
proxy conditions around macro-social affective climate. The contribution is not a validated ranking of
countries. The contribution is a construct map and transparent workflow that show how candidate
indicators can be assigned to conceptual layers, normalized, decomposed and tested for sensitivity.

The next research step is to strengthen the construct validity of STI by adding direct trust, cohesion,
legitimacy and emotional-climate indicators, then testing whether STI dimensions converge with
