Abstract
This paper proposes the Gradient of Knowing (GoK-8), an integrative conceptual framework for interpreting
evidence of knowing across learning, practice and contribution. GoK-8 distinguishes eight descriptors:
Unawareness, Knowledge, Understanding, Controlled Tryout / Demonstration, Accountable Practice, Mastery,
Creative / Adaptive Expertise, and Founding / Field-Building. The framework synthesizes established
traditions in educational objectives, competence assessment, skill acquisition, structural learning outcomes,
adaptive expertise, learning progressions and rubric-based assessment.
The central contribution is not a claim of empirical validation. Rather, GoK-8 is presented as a diagnostic map
and rubric candidate for distinguishing forms of evidence that are often collapsed under the single term
"knowing." The model is especially concerned with the difference between controlled demonstration and
accountable real-world practice, and with the difference between routine mastery and adaptive or creative
performance. Stage-model critiques require caution: GoK-8 should not be interpreted as a deterministic
universal ladder. Its levels are intended as heuristic, domain-specific and empirically revisable descriptors. The
eighth descriptor, Founding / Field-Building, is retained as a provisional trans-individual extension because it
depends on adoption by others rather than individual competence alone.
The paper concludes with a validation agenda. Future work should include expert review, artifact sampling in a
concrete pilot domain, rater training, inter-rater reliability assessment, comparison to competing models and
evaluation of instructional usefulness. Scientific-writing development is proposed as a suitable first pilot
domain because it produces observable artifacts across multiple levels: notes, literature matrices, outlines,
drafts, revisions and submission packages.
Keywords: learning progression; competence assessment; rubric; mastery; adaptive expertise; professional
development; scientific writing; conceptual framework.

1. Introduction
Educational and professional-development systems regularly face a practical question: what does it mean for a
person to know something well enough to act with it? The answer is rarely simple. A person may recognize
terminology without understanding relationships. Another may explain a concept clearly but fail to perform a
task under real constraints. A learner may succeed in a simulation but not in accountable practice. A
practitioner may perform reliably in routine cases but struggle when the situation changes. An expert may
produce novel work, but novelty alone does not guarantee adaptive quality.
Despite these distinctions, educational language often treats "knowing" as a single state. This creates
assessment errors. A quiz score can be mistaken for competence. A demonstration can be mistaken for real
practice. A single successful performance can be mistaken for mastery. A novel output can be mistaken for
creativity even when it lacks quality or fit. These confusions matter in schools, universities, professional
training, workplace development and scientific writing.

The Gradient of Knowing (GoK-8) is proposed as a practical framework for separating these states. It uses
eight descriptors:
1. Unawareness
2. Knowledge
3. Understanding
4. Controlled Tryout / Demonstration
5. Accountable Practice
6. Mastery
7. Creative / Adaptive Expertise
8. Founding / Field-Building
GoK-8 is not intended to replace existing models. It is intended to integrate several partial perspectives into one
diagnostic language. Bloom's taxonomy and its revision help formulate cognitive objectives. Miller's pyramid
helps distinguish knowing, showing and doing. Skill-acquisition models describe movement toward fluent
performance. SOLO taxonomy highlights the structural quality of understanding. Adaptive-expertise literature
distinguishes routine fluency from flexible innovation. Learning-progression and rubric literature define the
standard for future validation.
The research question guiding this paper is: Can an eight-level Gradient of Knowing model integrate cognitive
understanding, controlled tryout, real-world practice, mastery, creativity and field-building into a useful
learning-progression framework and assessment rubric?
The answer offered here is provisional. GoK-8 has conceptual value as a framework and rubric candidate. It is
not yet a validated theory. The purpose of this paper is therefore to define the model, locate it against
established frameworks, identify its limitations and outline a validation agenda.

2. Related Literature
2.1 Cognitive Objectives
Bloom's taxonomy and the revised taxonomy provide a widely used language for educational objectives. Their
main value for GoK-8 is in distinguishing early cognitive evidence: recognition, recall, understanding,
application and creation. They help describe why factual knowledge is not the same as explanation or creative
production.
However, cognitive taxonomies do not by themselves solve the problem of accountable performance. A learner
can meet a cognitive objective in a classroom setting while still lacking the ability to act in real conditions.
GoK-8 therefore treats cognitive evidence as necessary but not sufficient for practice-oriented competence.

2.2 Competence Assessment
Miller's pyramid is central to the GoK-8 distinction between controlled tryout and accountable practice. The
broad Miller distinction between knowing, knowing how, showing how and doing supports the idea that
evidence changes when assessment moves from knowledge to demonstration to real performance. In GoK-8,
Controlled Tryout / Demonstration describes bounded, guided or simulated performance. Accountable Practice
describes performance in real contexts where constraints, stakeholders and consequences are present.
This distinction is one of the most important practical contributions of GoK-8. Many training systems
overvalue simulated demonstration because it is easier to observe and score. GoK-8 makes that limitation
explicit: demonstration is useful evidence, but it is not the same as accountable practice.

Skill-acquisition models such as Dreyfus and Dreyfus and Fitts-Posner provide comparison points for
movement from rule-guided action toward fluent performance. These models support the general idea that
competence can change qualitatively through experience and feedback. They also support the distinction
between early controlled action and later reliable performance.
At the same time, stage-model critiques require caution. Professional development is not simply a universal
movement through fixed stages. It is contextual, embodied, domain-specific and often nonlinear. A person may
be a master in one domain and a beginner in another. A learner may show advanced understanding but weak
practice, or strong routine performance but limited adaptivity. GoK-8 therefore should be read as an evidence
map, not as a deterministic ladder.

2.4 Structural Understanding
SOLO taxonomy is useful for defining Understanding. In GoK-8, understanding is not treated as confidence,
fluency or repetition. It requires observable structural evidence: explaining relationships, comparing cases,
giving reasons and transferring explanation to nearby examples. This helps avoid a common assessment
problem: mistaking fluent vocabulary for conceptual structure.

2.5 Adaptive Expertise And Creativity
The transition from Mastery to Creative / Adaptive Expertise is a major conceptual boundary. Routine mastery
means stable, reliable performance across varied cases. Adaptive expertise requires more: the ability to modify
methods, transfer across contexts and create novel solutions while preserving quality.
For this reason, GoK-8 does not equate creativity with novelty alone. A new solution can be poor, irrelevant or
inefficient. Creative evidence must show adaptive fit, conceptual grounding and quality. This distinction is
important in scientific work, software engineering, design, education and professional practice.

2.6 Learning Progressions And Rubrics
Learning-progression literature supports cautious positioning. A proposed progression needs more than an
intuitive sequence. It needs conceptual analysis, empirical recovery, comparison to competing models and
evidence of instructional usefulness. For GoK-8, this means the current paper can propose a framework, but it
cannot claim that learners empirically move through these levels as a universal pattern.
Rubric literature adds another constraint. Moskal and Leydens emphasize that rubric validity depends on the
intended purpose of assessment and on whether scoring criteria address relevant content, constructs and
criterion-related performance. They also note that a well-designed rubric cannot compensate for a poorly
designed assessment task. Reliability requires attention to scorer consistency, clear categories, anchor examples
and rater discussion. McHugh's discussion of kappa reinforces the need to measure agreement when multiple
raters classify evidence. Percent agreement can be useful but may overestimate agreement because it does not
account for chance.
These sources imply that GoK-8 cannot be considered a valid or reliable assessment tool merely because the
level labels are plausible. Validation must be designed and executed.

3. The GoK-8 Framework
GoK-8 interprets knowing through four assessment dimensions:
 • cognitive structure: what the learner can recognize, explain and relate;
 • performance context: whether action occurs in recall, simulation, real-world work or variable contexts;
 • accountability: whether outcomes have consequences for stakeholders;
 • generativity: whether the learner can adapt, create, teach, standardize or build durable practice.

Levels 1-7 describe individual learning and performance evidence. Level 8 is different. It depends on adoption

the individual act.

4. Proposed Validation Agenda
GoK-8 requires validation before it can be used as an assessment instrument. The validation agenda should
proceed in stages.
First, expert content review should examine whether the level descriptors are clear, relevant and
non-overlapping. Experts should include people with experience in learning assessment, professional
development and the selected pilot domain. They should evaluate whether each level has meaningful indicators
and whether any level mixes multiple constructs.
Second, a pilot domain should be selected. Scientific-writing development is proposed as the first domain
because it produces observable artifacts across levels. A novice may be unable to define basic research terms. A
learner may collect sources but fail to synthesize them. Another may draft an introduction under guidance. A
more advanced writer may produce a full manuscript with accountable claims, source verification and
revisions. A highly developed writer may create new frameworks or methods that others adopt.

Third, an artifact sample should be created. Possible artifact types include notes, literature matrices, research
briefs, outlines, manuscript drafts, revision histories, response-to-reviewer tables and submission packages. The
sample should remove personal or sensitive information.
Fourth, raters should be trained. At least two independent raters should score calibration artifacts, discuss
disagreements and refine the scoring guide before formal scoring. Anchor examples should be created for each
level where possible.
Fifth, inter-rater reliability should be estimated. If each artifact receives one ordinal level, weighted kappa may
be appropriate. If raters assign numeric scores across dimensions, an intraclass-correlation approach may be
considered. Percent agreement can be reported descriptively, but it should not be the only reliability evidence.
Sixth, GoK-8 should be compared to competing models. Possible alternatives include a Bloom-only
classification, a Miller-only competence model or a simpler four-level progression. If GoK-8 does not improve
interpretation beyond these alternatives, its scientific value is weaker.
Finally, instructional usefulness should be tested. A framework is more valuable if it improves feedback,
self-assessment, mentoring decisions or revision quality. This is especially important if GoK-8 is used as a
formative tool rather than a grading instrument.

5. Discussion
GoK-8's strongest contribution is integrative. It brings together cognitive objectives, competence assessment,
skill acquisition, structural understanding, adaptive expertise and rubric validation into one practical map. Its
most useful distinction may be between Controlled Tryout / Demonstration and Accountable Practice. This
distinction is common in professional life but often weakly represented in ordinary educational language.
The framework also clarifies the difference between mastery and adaptive creativity. A master can perform
reliably in many situations. A creative or adaptive expert can modify methods and create fit-for-purpose
solutions when routine patterns are insufficient. This matters in domains where change is constant, such as
scientific work, software engineering, organizational development and design.
The model is also intentionally cautious about field-building. Founding / Field-Building is not simply "being
very good." It requires adoption evidence by others. This makes the level harder to assign, but it prevents
premature self-attribution. A person cannot simply claim to be at level 8; the evidence must include durable
uptake.
There are important risks. First, GoK-8 may be mistaken for another rigid stage model. This paper rejects that
interpretation. Levels are heuristic descriptors of evidence. They may vary across domains, and learners may
show mixed profiles across dimensions. Second, GoK-8 may appear too similar to existing models. Its defense
depends on whether the integration improves assessment language and development decisions. Third, without
empirical validation, GoK-8 remains a conceptual framework and rubric candidate.
The current paper is therefore best read as an invitation to validation. It defines a model, identifies its
relationship to existing literature and makes its limitations explicit.

6. Limitations
This paper does not present empirical validation. It does not show that learners move through GoK-8 levels in a
fixed order. It does not establish inter-rater reliability. It does not prove predictive validity or instructional
effectiveness.
Several source claims remain access-limited. Some foundational sources are verified through bibliographic
records or publisher metadata but still require full-text close-reading before final journal submission. The
current draft avoids detailed claims from sources that were not fully accessible in this pass.
The level labels also require further refinement. Controlled Tryout / Demonstration is a workable English label
for level 4, but it may need terminological testing. Founding / Field-Building remains the most speculative

7. Conclusion
GoK-8 offers a structured vocabulary for distinguishing forms of knowing that are often conflated: recognition,
explanation, controlled performance, accountable practice, mastery, adaptive creativity and field-building
contribution. Its current value is conceptual and practical. It can help identify what kind of evidence is present,
what kind is missing and what transition blockers may prevent development.
The next step is validation. A scientific-writing pilot can test whether trained raters can apply the rubric
consistently, whether GoK-8 adds interpretive value beyond simpler models and whether GoK-8-guided
feedback improves development decisions. Until then, GoK-8 should be presented as a proposed framework
and validation agenda, not as a validated theory.
