# I Built a GPT That Thinks Like a Portrait Artist

![Original photo and artistic interpretation](../../../../assets/publications/medium-9b909c8e1241/1528d50b48d00031.webp)


Original photo and artistic interpretation. The assistant goes beyond style transfer, rethinking the angle, background, and visual approach.

Sometimes ideas don’t come from a roadmap.
They come from a feeling.

I once stumbled upon a simple prompt that turned a photograph into a pencil sketch — as if someone had carefully drawn it in a notebook. No flashy effects, no obvious stylization. It didn’t feel like a filter. It felt like someone’s work: attentive, restrained, human.

That moment sparked a thought.
If a model can already transfer facial features with this level of care, maybe it can be used not just to “draw in a style,” but to behave like an artist.

I wanted to shift the goal entirely.
Not to replicate a photo, and not to chase photorealism, but to approach the process the way a portrait artist would in a studio: observe the person, sense the mood of the image, understand what matters, what can be emphasized, and what should quietly disappear. First comes an internal image — an intuition of the final work — and only then does technique become relevant.

That idea became the foundation for the assistant I built.

It doesn’t start by generating an image.
It starts by looking.

Given a photograph, it proposes several artistic directions that feel appropriate for this person and this moment. Not a random menu of styles, but different ways the portrait could speak: softer or more restrained, airy or dramatic, quiet or structured.

From there, it helps shape the presentation.
Should the background be simplified?
Would a different format feel more balanced?
Where should attention rest — and where should it fade?

Sometimes it suggests subtle compositional changes, not for effect, but for clarity. None of this is forced. You can accept the suggestions, ignore them, or guide the process yourself. The assistant behaves less like a command-driven tool and more like a thoughtful collaborator.

Once a direction is agreed upon, it generates the artwork.

And the process doesn’t end there.

If something feels off — if you want it calmer, stronger, more minimal, more expressive — the assistant takes that feedback and translates it into artistic options. It doesn’t simply “apply changes.” It proposes ways to realize your intent while preserving the integrity of the piece, much like a painter responding to a client’s comment in a studio.

One unexpected aspect I really enjoyed was the sense of exploration.
At times, the assistant suggests approaches I wasn’t even familiar with. In one case, it proposed suibokuga (sumi-e) — traditional Japanese monochrome ink painting. I agreed out of curiosity, and the result was surprisingly precise and expressive. The technique didn’t decorate the portrait; it revealed something essential about it.

Throughout the design, preserving identity mattered deeply to me.
A portrait can be idealized, simplified, or stylized — but it shouldn’t lose the person. The assistant keeps facial structure and key features intact, while moving artistic expression into light, tone, texture, and composition. Even hair can be simplified or rearranged for clarity, as long as the person remains unmistakably themselves.

I also wanted the experience to be frictionless.
To begin, you simply upload a photo. The assistant evaluates it and immediately proposes artistic options.

In the end, this became something more than an image generator or a style tool.
It’s a virtual portraitist — a calm, reflective assistant for thoughtful creative work. You can use it to create a portrait, explore unfamiliar artistic languages, or simply see a familiar image from a new perspective.
